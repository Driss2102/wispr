Email is the ideal candidate for dictation: it is almost entirely prose, it is high volume, and most replies are short. If you only adopt voice for one task, make it your inbox. Here is a routine that works, plus the mistakes that trip people up.

## Why email fits dictation so well

Unlike code or spreadsheets, email is just sentences. Modern dictation adds punctuation, drops filler, and can match a professional tone, so a spoken reply reads like a written one. The result is that you answer at the speed you think, not the speed you type.

There is a second reason: email is repetitive. You write the same kinds of replies all day, so any speed gain compounds across dozens of messages. Shaving thirty seconds off each reply adds up fast when you send forty a day.

## A simple routine

1. **Triage first, dictate second.** Scan the inbox and decide what needs a reply. Do not dictate while deciding; separate the thinking from the talking.
2. **Dictate the reply in one pass.** Say the whole message start to finish; do not stop to fix small things. Momentum is the point.
3. **Review, then send.** Fix names, numbers, and links by keyboard. Everything else is usually fine on the first pass.

That is the entire method. The discipline is in step two: resist the urge to correct mid-sentence, because stopping and starting is what makes dictation feel slow.

## Handling the common reply types

- **Quick acknowledgements.** "Thanks, got it, I will have this to you by Thursday." One breath, done.
- **Longer explanations.** Talk it through as if the person were on the phone, then tighten the result. Spoken explanations are often clearer than typed ones because you naturally simplify.
- **Templated replies.** For messages you send constantly, a text snippet beats dictation. Combine the two: trigger the snippet, then dictate the one custom line. See [Wispr Flow snippets](/docs/guides/wispr-flow-snippets/).

## Keep it human

Dictated email can actually sound warmer than typed email, because you talk the way you speak to people. Do not over-format. A quick, clear, spoken reply often lands better than a stiff typed one. If anything, the risk is sounding too casual, so keep your tone setting appropriate for work and read the first line back before sending.

## Watch-outs

- Check names and figures before sending; those are where dictation slips.
- In an open office, keep it quiet or step away. See [dictation etiquette in open offices](/blog/dictation-in-open-offices/).
- Sensitive content: remember dictation is processed in the cloud by default, so read [cloud vs on-device dictation](/blog/cloud-vs-on-device-dictation/) if you handle confidential mail.

## Make it stick

Give it one week on email only. Do not add other tasks yet. By day five, replying by voice will feel more natural than typing, and that single habit is usually what convinces people to use voice everywhere else.

## FAQ

**Is it faster to dictate email than type it?**
For most people, yes, because email is prose and replies are short. The gain is largest on longer messages and acknowledgements you would otherwise type out in full.

**How do I dictate email without sounding robotic?**
Talk the way you would speak to the person, dictate in one pass, and keep a professional tone setting. Spoken email tends to read warmer, not colder.

**Can I dictate email on my phone?**
Yes. The phone is often the best place to dictate replies, since talking to a phone feels natural and you can step somewhere quiet.
